A Lightweight Transformer-Based Method for Short Text Classification

Abstract
Short text classification is difficult because posts, comments, and short messages contain limited context, informal language, and ambiguous references. This paper presents LiteText, a compact transformer-based classification method designed for resource-constrained academic and service applications. LiteText combines a pretrained language encoder with a small task-specific attention pooling layer and a two-layer classification head. The design aims to retain contextual representations while reducing the number of trainable parameters during task adaptation. We evaluate the method on three public short-text benchmarks covering sentiment, topic, and intent classification. In the reported experiments, LiteText improves macro-F1 over a bag-of-words baseline and a bidirectional recurrent baseline, while remaining close to a full fine-tuning reference. The paper also studies sequence truncation, class imbalance, and inference cost. The findings suggest that parameter-efficient adaptation can be useful when labelled examples and compute are limited. The experiments are controlled benchmark evaluations; they do not establish performance in every production environment.

1. Introduction
Short messages are common in product feedback, help-desk requests, social platforms, and mobile applications. Automatically assigning a label to these messages can support search, triage, analytics, and moderation. However, a short message may omit the subject of a sentence, rely on conversational context, or use spelling variations and abbreviations. Traditional bag-of-words models are efficient but treat words largely as independent features and may fail to represent context. Recurrent neural networks can model token order, but their sequential computation can limit throughput and their representations may be less effective when training data is small.

Transformer encoders use self-attention to represent relationships among tokens. Pretrained encoders can transfer linguistic knowledge to a new classification task, but updating every model parameter can be expensive and may overfit small datasets. This work investigates a smaller adaptation strategy for short-text classification. The research question is whether a frozen pretrained encoder, combined with a lightweight trainable pooling and classification module, can provide competitive benchmark performance with fewer trainable parameters.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it defines LiteText, a compact architecture that uses contextual token representations and attention pooling. Second, it compares the method with conventional and neural baselines across three task types. Third, it examines how truncation length, class imbalance, and inference cost affect the observed results. The intended contribution is an empirical study of a practical model configuration rather than a claim that one architecture is universally optimal.

2. Method
LiteText begins with a pretrained transformer encoder. Input text is normalized by lowercasing where appropriate, preserving punctuation, and tokenizing with the encoder's tokenizer. A special classification token and separator tokens follow the tokenizer's standard convention. The sequence is padded or truncated to a maximum length of 64 tokens in the main experiment. The encoder weights remain frozen during task adaptation, so gradients are not used to update its parameters.

Let the final encoder output be a sequence of contextual vectors. A trainable attention pooling layer computes a scalar relevance score for each token vector, normalizes the scores across non-padding positions, and forms a weighted sum. The pooled vector is passed through dropout and a two-layer feed-forward classification head with a nonlinear activation. The output layer produces one logit per class. Softmax converts logits to class probabilities at inference time. Padding positions are excluded from attention pooling using the attention mask.

The trainable components are the attention pooling layer and classification head. This separation reduces the number of updated parameters compared with full encoder fine-tuning. It also means that the method depends on the pretrained encoder already containing useful representations for the target language. The model is trained with cross-entropy loss. The optimizer, learning rate, batch size, and dropout are selected on the validation split. Early stopping monitors validation macro-F1 to reduce overfitting. The same preprocessing and data splits are used for all compared methods.

3. Experiments
The evaluation uses three public benchmark datasets. The first is a short-message sentiment dataset with positive, negative, and neutral labels. The second is a topic classification dataset containing short user-generated statements assigned to topic categories. The third is an intent dataset in which short requests are mapped to service intents. The paper uses the official train, validation, and test partitions where supplied. When a benchmark does not provide a validation partition, a stratified portion of the training data is reserved for model selection. Exact dataset release identifiers and sample counts are recorded in the accompanying experiment configuration, rather than inferred from dataset names.

We compare LiteText with a bag-of-words logistic regression classifier, a bidirectional gated recurrent unit (BiGRU) classifier with pretrained word vectors, and a full fine-tuning transformer reference. The bag-of-words baseline uses unigram and bigram features. The BiGRU baseline uses the same text normalization and a fixed embedding initialization. The full fine-tuning reference updates all encoder and classification parameters. Hyperparameters are selected using validation data, and the test set is used only for final reporting.

Macro-F1 is the primary metric because it gives equal weight to each class and is informative when class frequencies differ. Accuracy is reported as a secondary metric. We also record trainable parameter count and average inference latency per example under the same hardware and batch configuration. Latency measurements are intended for relative comparison within this experiment; they should not be interpreted as universal device-independent timings. Each model is run with multiple random seeds, and the reported comparison uses the mean across runs.

4. Results
Across the three benchmarks, LiteText obtains higher macro-F1 than the bag-of-words and BiGRU baselines in the reported evaluation. The full fine-tuning transformer achieves the strongest or statistically comparable macro-F1 depending on the dataset, while requiring more trainable parameters. LiteText therefore occupies a middle ground: it provides contextual features and a compact adaptation head without updating the entire encoder. Accuracy follows a similar overall pattern, although the difference between methods is smaller on the topic dataset.

The sequence-length ablation compares maximum lengths of 32, 64, and 128 tokens. The 64-token setting gives the best average validation macro-F1 in the reported configuration. Shorter sequences sometimes remove useful context, while longer sequences increase computation and do not consistently improve scores. The class-imbalance experiment compares ordinary cross-entropy with class-weighted cross-entropy. Weighting improves minority-class recall on the most imbalanced benchmark, but may slightly reduce precision for frequent classes. The paper consequently recommends selecting the weighting strategy using validation metrics rather than applying it automatically.

The parameter analysis confirms that LiteText updates substantially fewer parameters than the full fine-tuning reference because the transformer encoder is frozen. Its measured inference latency is lower than the full fine-tuning reference in the reported setup, though the encoder still contributes most of the forward-pass cost. These results support the claim that the adaptation head is parameter-efficient; they do not show that the entire model is inexpensive on every device. 5. Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation covers only three public benchmarks, so results may not transfer to specialized domains, languages, or message distributions. Second, freezing the encoder limits the model's ability to adapt when domain vocabulary differs substantially from pretraining data. Third, benchmark labels may contain noise or reflect annotation conventions that differ from operational requirements. Fourth, latency depends on hardware, software versions, batch size, and deployment configuration. The experiments do not include a long-term production deployment or a user study.

The method also depends on access to a suitable pretrained transformer. In settings with strict memory limits, even a frozen encoder may be too large. Future work could compare smaller distilled encoders, quantization, domain-adaptive pretraining, and calibration methods. Evaluation on multilingual and domain-specific datasets would help establish where the method is reliable. A deployment study should measure throughput, energy use, drift, and the consequences of classification errors.

6. Conclusion
This paper introduced LiteText, a lightweight transformer-based method for short-text classification that freezes a pretrained encoder and trains a compact attention pooling and classification head. On three public benchmarks, it outperforms the selected bag-of-words and BiGRU baselines in macro-F1 and remains competitive with a full fine-tuning reference while updating fewer parameters. Ablations indicate that sequence length and class weighting should be selected using validation data. The evidence supports parameter-efficient adaptation as a practical option for some short-text tasks, subject to domain, hardware, and dataset limitations. The paper does not claim universal superiority; broader evaluation is needed before deployment in safety-critical or high-impact settings.
